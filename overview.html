Every birding app tells you where. This one tells you when.

A forecasting tool for birders, and a logbook that teaches beginners what they are looking at. This page explains what problem it solves and how it was built.

Tharaka Jayalath 12 August 2026 8,129 checklists · 73 species github.com/taj771/birding-coach

Part one

What we are trying to address

Problem 1 — every tool answers where, none answers when

Traveling Birder, BirdTrip, BirdPlan and eBird's own Hotspot Explorer all take a target species and hand back a place. None of them says go at 6:30, not 10:00, or budget ninety minutes, not thirty.

Those two turn out to be the largest measurable effects in the data. A birder who drives to the right place at the wrong hour has wasted the morning, and nothing they own told them so.

Problem 2 — a beginner cannot name what they just saw

Someone new to the hobby sees a small brown bird, and that is the end of the encounter. They cannot look it up, cannot record it, and cannot learn from it. Field guides are organised by taxonomy, which is only useful to somebody who already knows roughly what they are holding.

Both problems have the same answer: knowing which birds are plausible here, right now. That single calculation is a forecast to an experienced birder and a shortlist to a beginner.

Part two

How we did it

Built

Planned

Ranking among other birders, to give logging a reason beyond record keeping. Display names are already collected for it; the screen is not written yet.

2.2  The identification helper, and why it is the forecast in disguise

This is the part worth understanding, because it is one calculation doing two jobs. The forecasting model can score every species at once, so pointing it at a birder's current location and time produces a ranked shortlist — and a shortlist with photographs is an identification tool.

01

Where and when

The birder drops a pin and sets a time. That is all the model needs.

02

The model ranks

All 73 species are scored for that spot, that hour, that week of the year — from what birders have historically reported there.

03

Shown as photographs

The top two dozen appear as pictures. A beginner is choosing between 24 plausible birds instead of hundreds.

04

The pick becomes data

What they choose is saved to their logbook — and marked uncertain, because a guess must not look like certainty.

It identifies nothing on its own. It narrows the field using other birders' records and lets a person decide — which is why it needs no image recognition, and why it gets better as the database grows.

2.3  Where the data comes from — and the problem of starting

A forecast built from user logs needs user logs. On day one there are none. That is the cold-start problem, and every app of this kind faces it.

So the model is trained on eBird — the Cornell Lab of Ornithology's global database, where birders have been submitting checklists for twenty years. Our own users' sightings are recorded from the first day and will supplement it; eBird is what makes the app useful before that happens.

Every outing logged in the app is stored in the same shape as an eBird checklist, deliberately — so it can join the training data rather than sit beside it.

Current constraint — awaiting approval

Training on the whole United States means the eBird Basic Dataset, the bulk download Cornell provides for research. That request is submitted and pending manual review, which typically takes about a week.

Until it clears, the working dataset is a single county — Allegheny County, Pennsylvania — assembled by scraping the eBird API directly. That is not a preference but arithmetic: the API costs two calls per checklist, which is about 4,650 for a month of one county, roughly 438,000 for a year of Pennsylvania, and over a million for a month of the United States. One county is what the API can honestly supply.

2.4  How the data is acquired and stored

eBird has no bulk endpoint on the API, so a checklist costs two calls: one to the day's feed to learn who went birding, and one per checklist to learn what they found.

01

Scrape, seven days behind

Deliberately not today. eBird submissions trail the outing — a weekend trip gets entered on Monday — so scraping fresh data captures only the people who log in the field, which is a biased sample. Seven days is enough for submissions to settle.

02

Store in a database file

DuckDB, a single file. Never committed to git — eBird records are not redistributable, so the repository must not contain them.

03

Accumulate day by day

The scheduled run adds a rolling window each morning, so the database grows on its own. Re-running costs nothing: rows are merged on their natural keys, so a day already stored is skipped rather than duplicated.

04

Refit monthly

New checklists arrive continuously, but how detection responds to hour, effort and weather does not move week to week. Scraping is daily; fitting is monthly.

05

Publish the coefficients

The finished model is a lookup table plus a sum, so the app needs no server and no Python — it fetches the numbers and does the arithmetic in the browser.

2.5  Why Hugging Face and GitHub, rather than a server

Three constraints decided this, and together they leave one sensible shape.

It has to run without a laptop. A daily 08:00 scrape cannot depend on someone's machine being awake. GitHub Actions runs it on a schedule, free for public repositories.

It has to remember between runs. A cloud runner starts with an empty disk every time, and the data cannot live in git for licensing reasons. A private Hugging Face dataset holds the database: each run pulls it, adds what it scraped, pushes it back.

The app has to read the model without credentials. Raw eBird records may not be redistributed — but a fitted coefficient is derived work, a number describing a pattern rather than a copy of anybody's checklist. So those go to a second, public dataset that the browser reads straight from a CDN.

2.6  The model

Everything above exists to make one calculation possible, and it rests on a single property of eBird data.

A birder can mark a checklist complete — a declaration that they listed every species they could identify. On a complete checklist, a species that does not appear is evidence the bird was not detected. It is not missing data. It is a verified zero.

That is what makes a probability computable. A pile of sightings gives you a numerator and nothing else. Complete checklists give you the denominator: out of 412 complete lists submitted at Frick Park in week 31, how many contained a Wood Thrush? Now you can divide.

The method is logistic regression — the standard tool for modelling a yes-or-no outcome — fitted separately for each of the 73 species. It estimates the chance a species is reported, given five blocks of information:

Effort
duration · distance · observers · travelling How hard you looked. The largest effect in the data — and not optional. Rain makes birders cut a walk short and makes birds quieter. Leave effort out and the rain coefficient absorbs both, so you would publish “rain lowers your chances” when half of that is “rain shortened your walk”.
Time of day
one term per hour, 06:00–20:00 Dawn chorus and the midday lull. Strongly species-dependent — generic “go early” advice underdelivers.
Season
one term per week of the year Migration, breeding song, winter absence. Indexed by day-of-year rather than date, so last August's checklists inform this August.
Site
one term per hotspot Habitat, compressed into a single number. Penalised fitting is what makes this possible: a site visited 89 times keeps its own estimate, one visited four times is pulled toward the county average.
Weather
wind · temperature · precipitation · cloud Kept as a separable block, because it is the thing actually under test — and the only block that can be known in advance for a future date.

What is not done

Two coverage gaps are visible in the data itself, and worth showing rather than describing.

Ask about a week with no term and the model has nothing seasonal to say — so the app reports that rather than guessing. A historical backfill is running to close this. Autumn migration is the conspicuous absence.

Owling and nocturnal migration are outside what this can answer. Few enough checklists are submitted at night that a term would be fitted on almost nothing.

To do

  • Get the eBird Basic Dataset approved and retrain on it. This is the one that unblocks everything else — more counties, more weeks, more species.
  • Finish the historical backfill so the seasonal term covers the whole calendar rather than 18 weeks of it.
  • Feed app logbooks back into the model. Sightings are being recorded in the right shape, but nothing yet carries them from the app's database into the training data. The sign-in page already promises this, so it is a promise outstanding rather than a nice-to-have.
  • Build the leaderboard. Display names are collected; the screen is not written.
  • Photo upload. Deliberately deferred — storing images is easy, reviewing them is not, and an unreviewed upload path is a moderation problem waiting.

Stated limits

  • Detection, not presence. This predicts whether you would report a bird, not whether one is there. For a birder that is the right target. It is not the same quantity, and the distinction matters to anyone reading these numbers ecologically.
  • One county, until the bulk dataset is approved.
  • Effort is endogenous. Birders extend a good outing, so duration is partly a consequence of success rather than only a cause. The effort coefficient is inflated by an unknown amount.
  • Weather sits on a 25 km grid, which attenuates its coefficients toward zero. The wind effect is probably understated.
  • Observers self-select on weather. Nobody birds in a storm, so the observed weather range is truncated and whoever does go out is unusual.
  • The eBird feed truncates any county-day above 200 checklists, under-sampling exactly the busiest spring mornings.
